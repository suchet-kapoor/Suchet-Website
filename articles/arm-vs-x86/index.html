ARM vs x64: Which Processor Should You Choose?

Two chips, one decision. Hover or tap the illustrated cards as you read to watch each architecture work.

Introduction

In the year 1965, Intel and IBM were the R&D and innovation kings. Nobody even came close to them. They made the moon landing happen on the 8080 processor, just a small memory and their research.

But there was another company, BBC Radio, which was having a blast selling radios across England. But they had a major challenge. They were not able to sell more... the reason, as usual as of today... a chip shortage. There were just not enough powerful chips for them to make and sell.

So 2 minds — a hardware and a software engineer — joined hands and gave the world the first Intel x86 alternative — and named it ARM.

Today ARM powers Apple, our mobile phones, and half the world is deployed on AWS EC2, which runs on AWS Graviton chips powered by ARM 256-bit processors.

ARM solved one of the major challenges of Intel at that time: reducing watt/energy usage, increased speed, and efficient utilization of memory. Based on a RISC processor.

The Creators: Who Built ARM?

The two researchers behind ARM are Sophie Wilson and Steve Furber. They worked at Acorn Computers, and their work on the BBC Micro project led to the creation of the ARM architecture. While the BBC Radio connection is part of the broader context, it's the collaboration between Wilson and Furber that gave us the first ARM processor.

ARM initially had only 32 instruction sets compared to Intel's 386 (the x86 family grew to around 384 instructions at its peak).

The Architecture: CISC vs RISC

Intel x86 will load instructions 1 by 1 and often leaves half of the processing capacity empty. ARM, on the other hand, will load the full instruction into memory, which makes it easy to execute and has fewer instructions.

The key insight is simple: ARM loads fewer instructions into memory to complete the same task. With the rise of faster, cheaper memory chips, memory stopped being the bottleneck. Suddenly, the architecture that spent fewer cycles fetching instructions became the natural winner.

When to Choose x64

Single-Threaded Workloads

The flip side: x64 still wins for single-threaded tasks. Some workloads depend heavily on a single, fast core — gaming servers, one-off database transactions, high-frequency trading, and certain legacy applications are examples.

x86's per-core performance advantage means each individual operation streams through faster. If your task is dominated by a single thread that isn't waiting on I/O, x64 will often outperform ARM despite ARM's energy efficiency.

Legacy Software

Another reason to choose x64: compatibility. A huge ecosystem still assumes x86 architecture. If your application has native binaries compiled specifically for x86, porting to ARM means recompiling and retesting — which isn't always trivial.

When to Choose ARM

Data Centers and Cloud

ARM became wildly successful in data centers because cost and energy matter more than anything else:

  • Lower power draw = lower electricity bills
  • Less cooling required = lower infrastructure costs
  • Comparable or better throughput per dollar = better total cost of ownership

For modern cloud providers, ARM-based instances (like AWS Graviton, Google Axion, and Microsoft Cobalt) deliver the same workload for less money, with the added benefit of improved energy efficiency.

Long-Running Tasks

For long-duration tasks — batch processing, analytics workloads, API servers running continuously — ARM shines. Consider this example query:

SELECT * FROM users WHERE id = 12345;

On an ARM-based instance, this query runs approximately 0.2 milliseconds faster than on the same-size x64 instance. That doesn't sound like much — but for a query running millions of times a day across thousands of instances, that adds up to hours of computation saved and significant cost reduction.

More importantly, ARM's architecture favors throughput-heavy workloads where many cores work in parallel. Its design, with more cores available for the same price, handles high-concurrency server workloads exceptionally well.

Embedded and Mobile

ARM's success story has always been in embedded systems and mobile. From smartphones to IoT devices, the one-tenth power draw and memory efficiency make it the only sensible choice.
